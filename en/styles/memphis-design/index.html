Memphis Design

孟菲斯 · Memphis Group · Memphis Style

The postmodern language of the 1981 Milan Memphis Group: zigzag/polka-dot/checkerboard geometric patterning, deliberately clashing turquoise×coral×yellow×black, and flat geometry with thick black outlines (circles/triangles/squiggles) that refuses to harmonize.

Code breakdown

Structure & DOM

Memphis is a “pattern field + card” two-layer build: the whole scene is a cream laminate field, black-outlined motifs scatter asymmetrically across it (a decorative layer), and one hard-offset-shadow action card floats on top — the motifs never yield to text; the card stands out via hard shadow and outline alone.

<div class="scene">
  <svg class="pattern" aria-hidden="true"><!-- squiggles/zigzags/dots --></svg>
  <div class="card">
    <h2>Headline (outlined)</h2>
    <button class="btn">Shuffle ↻</button>
  </div>
</div>

Key points:

  • All motifs are inline SVG (path/polyline/circle), aria-hidden decoration with pointer-events: none;
  • The card outline and its hard offset shadow (a 0-blur shadow at 2× stroke width) share one black — thicken the outline and the shadow follows;
  • Motif placement uses deterministic pseudo-random (integer-hashed slots): the same seed reproduces the same layout, no flicker.

Styling & Parameters

Four parameters: one CSS variable, three JS-type params (re-laid on store change):

.scene {                       /* classic arrangement: the four hues verbatim from research 03 §13 */
  --mm-turquoise: #3eb8b0;     /* hexes are baseline settings; the research records no values */
  --mm-coral: #ff6f61;
  --mm-yellow: #ffd449;
  --mm-ink: #141414;
  --mm-cream: #f6f1e7;
}
.scene[data-palette="shuffle"] { --mm-a: coral; --mm-b: yellow; --mm-c: turquoise; }
.pattern circle,
.pattern polyline,
.pattern path { stroke-width: var(--mm-stroke); } /* outline weight, 3px baseline */
.card {
  border: var(--mm-stroke) solid var(--mm-ink);
  box-shadow: calc(var(--mm-stroke) * 2) calc(var(--mm-stroke) * 2) 0 var(--mm-ink);
}

Where the numbers come from:

  • “Pattern density & stacking layers, clashing palette (no-harmony constraint), stroke weight, motif library (squiggle/zigzag/dot), asymmetric composition” are all tunable variables listed verbatim in research 03 §13, none with recorded values — 3px stroke, density 8 and the four hexes are this entry’s baselines;
  • Palette discipline: only turquoise×coral×yellow×black; the “role swap” arrangement re-deals roles without adding a fifth hue — the correct reading of “no-harmony constraint”, not unlimited coloring;
  • The hard offset shadow is syntax shared with Neo-Brutalism (cf. this atlas’s neo-brutalism entry, 4px 4px 0 #000), but Memphis’ skeleton is the pattern while brutalism’s is the border.

Motion & Interaction

Memphis motion keeps a print-like flatness — the card pops on the pattern field with no long ease-out tails:

  • Entrance: the card lands with a 2° tilt over 0.45s cubic-bezier(0.22, 1, 0.36, 1), once;
  • Button feedback: hover grows the offset in reverse (translate: -1px -1px + longer shadow); :active presses the button “into” its shadow until it hits zero — squashing the hard shadow is the most honest press feedback this style has;
  • Shuffle: swaps to a new deterministic seed and re-lays motifs instantly (no transition) — like pulling another silkscreen print;
  • Under prefers-reduced-motion: reduce the entrance and button transitions stop; Shuffle still works instantly — motion is expendable, interaction is not.

Real-world examples

  • Carlton room divider (1981, Ettore Sottsass) — Memphis’ founding icon: zigzag shelving in clashing plastic laminate, the physical prototype of “pattern stacking + no-harmony color” (research 03 §13).
  • Miami Vice and Nickelodeon (1980s) — two recorded anchors of Memphis’ mid-80s-to-mid-90s spread through pop culture; the neon zigzags and color blocks remain its mass-memory carriers (research 03 §13).
  • Dior haute couture 2011-12 and Missoni 2015 — two high-profile fashion homages, evidence for the “limited collab / campaign page” usage rather than whole-site adoption (research 03 §13; enlighten is explicit that on the web it’s mostly brand assets/patterns/campaign pages).

Working ranges: stroke 2.5–4px, density 6–10. Motifs stay decorative; text and actions always live on solid cards. Any urge to add a fifth hue, a gradient or a blurred shadow strays off the no-harmony discipline.

When to use 3
  • Brand identity, event posters and social graphics — research 03 §13: on the web it's mostly brand assets/patterns/campaign pages, not whole-site language
  • Illustrated landing-page sections for young audiences (hero banners, section dividers, sticker badges)
  • Limited collabs and pop-ups riding the Gen-Z 80s revival — research 03 §13 records the revival as ongoing
When not to use 3
  • Body typography and data tables — pattern stacking buries text hierarchy and clashing hues fight scanning
  • Accessibility-sensitive contexts (health, government, banking) — the no-harmony palette discipline collides with 3:1/4.5:1 contrast rules
  • Whole-site saturation — enlighten is explicit that Memphis is a local language on the web; forcing it site-wide fatigues fast
Accessibility

Clashing hue pairings (coral on turquoise, coral on yellow) naturally fail contrast — every motif is aria-hidden decoration and carries no information; text always sits on solid cream/black fields checked for 4.5:1 against the worst backdrop (WCAG 2.1 SC 1.4.3); black outlines keep shape boundaries at 3:1 non-text contrast (SC 1.4.11); patterns are the only atmosphere carrier — with them off, the interface must still read.

Browser support

All motifs in the demo are inline SVG (path/polyline/circle) — support equals SVG 1.1, i.e. every modern browser; outlines are driven by the stroke-width attribute, no prefixes, no @supports branches. The CSS-variable injection path matches other entries.

Fallback

In extreme environments (SVG/images disabled) the pattern layer drops out entirely and the interface degrades to a static composition of cream field + black-outlined card + clashing color blocks — hierarchy and readability survive; only the visual density of pattern-stacking is lost.

Sources 3

OriginAn Italian design-and-architecture collective founded by Ettore Sottsass at a Milan gathering on 1980-12-06 — named while Bob Dylan's 'Stuck Inside of Mobile with the Memphis Blues Again' played on repeat — active 1980–1987; its debut caused a sensation at the 1981 Salone del Mobile with the Carlton room divider as the icon; Y2K took its baton in the late 90s and its graphic language fed vaporwave in the 2010s (research 03 §13).
